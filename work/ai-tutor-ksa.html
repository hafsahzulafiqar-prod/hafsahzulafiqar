Overview

  1. Problem

    Mualim needed an AI tutor for kids that felt natural, not bolted-on, for the Saudi Arabian market — a product two very different people had to trust at once: the child using it, and the parent deciding whether to let them.

  2. Central Question

    How do you design a single interface that feels safe, engaging, and genuinely useful to a child, while feeling transparent and trustworthy to the parent watching over them?

  3. Result

    Shipped an AI tutor experience that balances a child's engagement with a parent's trust — from first session through daily use, across web and mobile.

The full story

I was brought in as product design consultant, owning the work end to end: research, strategy, prototyping, and shipping. Mualim needed a tutor that made AI-driven learning approachable from the very first session, for kids as young as five.

Research

Designing for kids means designing for two audiences at once — the child actually using the product, and the parent or guardian deciding whether to trust it with their kid's learning. My UX research focused on understanding how each side needed to experience the product differently: what makes an AI tutor feel safe, engaging, and genuinely useful to a child, and what makes it feel trustworthy and transparent to the adult overseeing it. That split informed every decision that followed.

Designing for a child's attention and a parent's trust, at the same time — in a single interface.

Survey Findings

Alongside short usability sessions with kids, I ran a parent-facing survey to understand what would actually make an AI tutor feel safe enough to adopt — a child's enthusiasm meant nothing if the parent wouldn't approve the app in the first place.

The sessions with kids surfaced a different set of signals entirely: what kept their attention past the first few minutes, and exactly where they got stuck or gave up on a task.

Defining Personas

Two very different kinds of learners emerged, plus the parent watching over both — Mualim had to work for a young child just starting out, an independent older student, and the adult deciding whether to trust either of them with an AI tutor.

Y

Yusuf

8 · Early learner · Riyadh

The Curious Beginner

Goals
Feel like he's winning, not being tested; get instant, encouraging feedback.
Frustrations
Long blocks of text; tasks that feel like homework instead of play.
Decision drivers
Bright visuals, quick wins, a tutor that reacts to him personally.
“I want it to talk to me like it knows me, not like it's reading me a lesson.”
L

Lina

13 · Independent student · Jeddah

The Self-Directed Learner

Goals
Move at her own pace; skip what she already knows without having to ask.
Frustrations
Being treated like a much younger child; no real sense of mastery or progress.
Decision drivers
Autonomy, visible mastery tracking, a tutor that challenges rather than babies her.
“If it's too easy I'll just stop paying attention — I want to actually get somewhere.”
N

Noor

36 · Working parent · Riyadh

The Watching Guardian

Goals
Know her kids are actually learning, in plain language, without watching every session.
Frustrations
No visibility into what the AI is actually teaching; unclear if it's "good" screen time.
Decision drivers
Transparent progress, a sense of control, trust that the content is age-appropriate.
“I don't need to see every question — I need to know it's actually working.”

Project challenges

01 — Designing one interface for two audiences with opposite needs: a child who wants play and instant reward, and a parent who wants visible rigor and control — the same screen had to read differently to each of them.
02 — As an external consultant, I didn't own the AI tutor's underlying behavior, only the interface around it — every interaction had to be designed to feel consistent even when the model's responses varied.

Design considerations

We explored different design languages and approaches to see which would resonate with kids aged 5 to 15. We needed something that reinforced learning with positive emotions, without overcomplicating the experience — including directions that didn't work and were ruled out along the way.

Visual DNA

Warm, playful, and unmistakably built for kids — rounded shapes, bright accent colors, and a friendly tutor character carry the child-facing screens, while the parent-facing dashboard dials the same palette down to something calmer and more report-like.

Layout and Hierarchy

The child's view leads with what to do next — one big, obvious action at a time — while the parent's dashboard leads with a summary first, letting them drill into detail only if they want to.

Interactions

Every attempted answer gets an immediate, encouraging animation before moving on — no waiting, no ambiguity about whether it "counted." The tutor's help option is always one tap away, never buried behind a menu.

Core features

Three parts of the product carried the most weight in balancing a child's experience against a parent's trust.

The parent dashboard

One dashboard to check on their kids' progress — transparent enough to build trust without turning the child's learning into something that feels like it's being graded.

How it works

Progress surfaces to the parent in plain terms — what's been learned, what's next — without exposing every mistake or turning the dashboard into a report card.

Attempting challenges

Challenges were designed to be easy to navigate, reduce cognitive load, and encourage kids to try again if they got stuck — with the option to easily ask the AI tutor for help along the way.

How it works

A stuck child gets a low-friction path to help — asking the tutor directly — rather than a dead end that invites them to give up.

AI learning

Built around an unconventional approach to education — kids didn't need to memorize concepts, but to learn, unlearn, and relearn through iteration and goal-based learning.

How it works

Lessons pace around iteration rather than one-shot correctness — a wrong answer is a step in the process, not a failure state.

Strategy

With the research in hand, I shaped the product strategy around how an AI tutor should actually behave with a young learner: how it introduces itself, how it paces a lesson, how it responds when a child gets something wrong, and how it surfaces progress back to a parent without making the experience feel like it's being graded. My goal was a strategy that treated "AI-driven learning" as a promise to make good on through interaction design, not just a label on the product.
