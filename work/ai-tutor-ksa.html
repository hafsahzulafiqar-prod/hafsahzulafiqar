Overview

  1. Problem

    Mualim needed an AI tutor for kids that felt natural, not bolted-on, for the Saudi Arabian market: a product two very different people had to trust at once, the child using it, and the parent deciding whether to let them.

  2. Central Question

    How do you design a single interface that feels safe, engaging, and genuinely useful to a child, while feeling transparent and trustworthy to the parent watching over them?

  3. Result

    Shipped an AI tutor experience that balances a child's engagement with a parent's trust, from first session through daily use, across web and mobile.

The full story

I was brought in as product design consultant, owning the work end to end: research, strategy, prototyping, and shipping. Mualim needed a tutor that made AI-driven learning approachable from the very first session, for kids as young as five.

Part one

Designing for parents

Before a child ever opens the app, a parent has to decide the AI tutor is worth letting them use in the first place. This half of the work is about earning that trust: research, personas, and a core feature set built around making Mualim feel like one coherent platform a parent can rely on, not a black box.

Research findings

Designing for kids means designing for two audiences at once: the child actually using the product, and the parent or guardian deciding whether to trust it with their kid's learning. My UX research focused on understanding how each side needed to experience the product differently: what makes an AI tutor feel safe, engaging, and genuinely useful to a child, and what makes it feel trustworthy and transparent to the adult overseeing it. That split informed every decision that followed.

Designing for a child's attention and a parent's trust, at the same time, in a single interface.

A parent-facing survey ran alongside short usability sessions with kids to understand what would actually make an AI tutor feel safe enough to adopt. A child's enthusiasm meant nothing if the parent wouldn't approve the app in the first place.

Defining Personas

Two very different parent profiles emerged, from opposite ends of the trust spectrum: one already sold on AI-driven learning and just wanting visibility, the other openly skeptical of it.

N

Noor

36 · Working parent · Riyadh

The Watching Guardian

Goals
Know her kids are actually learning, in plain language, without watching every session.
Frustrations
No visibility into what the AI is actually teaching; unclear if it's "good" screen time.
Decision drivers
Transparent progress, a sense of control, trust that the content is age-appropriate.
“I don't need to see every question. I need to know it's actually working.”
K

Khalid

44 · First-time adopter · Jeddah

The Cautious Adopter

Goals
Confirm the AI is actually teaching correct material, not just entertaining; keep final say over what his kids see.
Frustrations
Marketing claims about "AI-powered learning" with no way to verify what's actually happening in a session.
Decision drivers
Transparency into content and sources, an easy way to pause or override the tutor, visible learning outcomes.
“I don't trust an algorithm to raise my kid. Show me exactly what it's teaching and I'll decide if it's good enough.”

Project challenges

01. Building trust without becoming another report card: parents needed real visibility, but too much detail risked making the product feel like surveillance rather than a helpful summary.
02. Converting skeptical, non-technical parents who distrust "AI-powered" as marketing language, without leaning on jargon or over-promising what the tutor could do.

Making an all-in-one platform

Parents didn't just want visibility into their child's learning. They wanted one place to manage all of it, instead of juggling a separate app or channel for progress, communication, and billing. That single idea shaped the whole parent experience: everything a parent needs sits behind one dashboard, not scattered across settings menus and support emails.

Core features

The parent dashboard is the anchor of that all-in-one platform. Everything below builds outward from it.

Multi-child dashboard

A parent with more than one child on Mualim sees all of them from a single screen, instead of switching accounts or apps to check on each one.

How it works

Each child gets their own card on the parent's home screen: progress, subject, and status at a glance, with a tap through to their full detail view.

Plain-language progress reports

Weekly summaries written in plain language, what's been learned, what's next, instead of raw scores that read like a report card.

Picture placeholder
How it works

Progress surfaces as short, plain-language summaries rather than exposing every individual answer or mistake.

Milestone & concern alerts

Parents are notified only when it actually matters, a milestone reached, or a topic their child is visibly stuck on, instead of a constant stream of activity notifications.

Picture placeholder
How it works

Alerts are threshold-based, not activity-based, so a parent's notifications stay meaningful instead of becoming noise they learn to ignore.

Manage plans & settings

Subscription, learning goals, and account controls all live in the same dashboard as progress. No separate billing portal or support ticket required.

Picture placeholder
How it works

Plan changes, payment details, and content controls sit one tap from the progress view they're already checking, instead of a separate settings destination.

Part two

Designing for students

Once a parent trusts the platform enough to let their child in, the goals flip entirely, from transparency and control to attention and encouragement. This half covers the actual learning experience: the interface a child spends real time in.

Project challenges

01. One interface had to work for a 6-year-old and a 14-year-old without feeling babyish to the older kid or overwhelming to the younger one.
02. Keeping a child's attention without gamifying so heavily that the actual learning got lost behind the rewards.

Design considerations

We explored different design languages and approaches to see which would resonate with kids aged 5 to 15. We needed something that reinforced learning with positive emotions, without overcomplicating the experience, including directions that didn't work and were ruled out along the way.

Easy to use interface

Mobile responsive

Visual DNA

Warm, playful, and unmistakably built for kids: rounded shapes, bright accent colors, and a friendly tutor character are meant to carry the child-facing screens. Real visuals for this section are still incoming.

Layout and Hierarchy

The child's view leads with what to do next, one big, obvious action at a time, rather than a menu of options to sort through. Real visuals for this section are still incoming.

AI learning made easy

Built around an unconventional approach to education: kids didn't need to memorize concepts, but to learn, unlearn, and relearn through iteration and goal-based learning. Lessons pace around iteration rather than one-shot correctness. A wrong answer is a step in the process, not a failure state.

How it works

A stuck child gets a low-friction path to help, asking the tutor directly, rather than a dead end that invites them to give up.

Positive reinforcement

Every attempted answer gets an immediate, encouraging reaction before moving on. No waiting, no ambiguity about whether it "counted." The goal was to reward effort and attempts, not just correct answers, so a wrong guess still feels like progress rather than a dead end. Real visuals for this section are still incoming.

Picture placeholder
How it works

A quick, positive animation follows every attempt, right or wrong, so the loop keeps a child moving forward instead of stalling on a mistake.

Final outcome

The shipped product balances what a child needs to stay engaged with what a parent needs to trust it: a single interface serving two audiences with very different relationships to the same learning experience.
